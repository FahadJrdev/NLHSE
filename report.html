Chapter 1 — Introduction

Opens wide: a PDE involves partial derivatives in several variables; a nonlinear evolution equation (NLEE) is a nonlinear PDE with time among its variables, written generally as ℱ(u, ut, uxx, uxxx, …, x, t) = 0. These describe fibre-optic wave dynamics, quantum mechanics, plasma physics, superconductivity and electromagnetic propagation.

It then narrows to the object of study — §1.2, the governing equation:

iUy + ½ (Uxx − Utt) + |U|²U = 0, i = √−1
(1)

U(x, y, t) is the wave envelope over two spatial variables and time. The report motivates it as a model for optical signal propagation in single-mode fibre.

§1.3 introduces the beta fractional derivative of Atangana et al., its formal limit definition (3) and five algebraic properties, then substitutes it into (1) to give the fractional form the project actually solves:

i ᴬ₀𝔇βyU + ½ ( ᴬ₀𝔇βxxU − ᴬ₀𝔇βttU ) + |U|²U = 0
(4)

§1.4 is the literature review: where NLSEs appear, and the precedent for the Unified Technique — specifically its use on the conformable time-fractional Schrödinger equation with perturbation terms.

Chapter 2 — Methodology

This chapter is deliberately generic: it states the Unified Technique for any nonlinear PDE, so that Chapter 3 can simply apply it. Four steps — travelling-wave transformation, series ansatz, coefficient matching, solve. It is covered in full on page 04.

Watch this seam

Chapter 2 presents the method as a general tool and cites reference [25] for it. Be ready to say clearly which parts of the report are the method (existing, borrowed, correctly attributed) and which are your contribution (applying it to this equation, under a beta derivative, and analysing the parameter dependence). Claiming the method itself would be an unforced error.

Chapter 3 — Solutions

The working chapter. The fractional travelling-wave transformation (12) converts the PDE into the ODE (13); the ODE separates into a real part (14) and an imaginary part (15); the imaginary part is solved immediately for the speed c (16); homogeneous balancing gives N = 1; the trial solution (17) is substituted; matching powers of δ(ξ) gives the algebraic system (18); Maple solves it for ω, A₀, A₁, B₁; and substituting back gives the two final solution families (19) and (20).

Chapter 5 — Conclusions

States that the Unified Technique proved effective for deriving soliton solutions, that the solutions illuminate wave behaviour under obliqueness, time and β variation, and that applications lie in mathematical physics, optical wave structures and wave transmission. It names three directions for future work: more generalised nonlinear equations, additional physical parameters, and — this is the sentence to remember — "employing numerical methods to verify the analytical results".

Use your own conclusion as the bridge

Your report already names numerical verification as the next step. That is the most useful sentence in the document for an interview: the move from analytical to numerical is not a change of direction being invented on the spot to please a panel — it is the continuation your own report proposed.

The spoken version

Say this — the ninety-second walkthrough

"The report has five chapters. Chapter 1 sets up the governing equation — the (2+1)-dimensional nonlinear hyperbolic Schrödinger equation, which models optical signal propagation in single-mode fibre — and introduces the beta fractional derivative, which generalises the integer-order derivatives with a parameter β between 0 and 1."

"Chapter 2 states the Unified Technique in general form: you assume a travelling-wave transformation, expand the solution as a finite series in an auxiliary function δ that satisfies a simple Riccati-type ODE, substitute, and match coefficients."

"Chapter 3 applies it. The transformation reduces the PDE to a single ODE in ξ. Separating real and imaginary parts gives the wave speed straight away. Balancing the cubic term against the second derivative gives N = 1, so the trial solution has three unknown constants. Matching powers of δ gives a nonlinear algebraic system, which I solved in Maple. Two solution families come out — hyperbolic when the auxiliary parameter χ is negative, trigonometric when it is positive."

"Chapter 4 plots them and studies the parameters: as the oblique angle increases the wave broadens and its amplitude falls; increasing β narrows the wave and raises its frequency while the amplitude holds. Chapter 5 concludes, and identifies numerical verification of the analytical results as the natural next step."
