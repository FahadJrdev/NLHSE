The Unified Technique

Four steps, and every one of them is a decision you should be able to defend. This is Chapter 2 of your report, stated generally, before it meets your equation.

In plain words

Nobody can solve a nonlinear PDE in general. There is no formula, and there never will be. So methods like this one do something more modest and much more practical: guess the shape of the answer, leave blanks in the guess, and let the equation fill the blanks in.

That is the whole idea. Everything below is the machinery for making the guess precise enough that the equation can fill it in.

The four moves

  1. Ride along with the wave. Assume the answer is a fixed shape moving at constant speed. Then instead of three variables (x, y, t) you have one — how far along the wave you are. A partial differential equation becomes an ordinary one, and ordinary ones are enormously easier.
  2. Write the answer as a short recipe. Guess that the shape is built out of one simple building-block function, called δ, combined a few ways: some of δ, some of 1 over δ, plus a constant. The amounts are unknown — those are the blanks.
  3. Substitute and compare. Put the guess into the equation. Because the building block was chosen so that its derivative is expressible in terms of itself, everything collapses into a polynomial in δ. A polynomial is zero only if every coefficient is zero — so you get one equation per power of δ. Blanks become a system of ordinary algebra.
  4. Solve the algebra. No calculus left. Hand it to Maple, get numbers back, put the numbers into the guess. Done.

Why δ is chosen so carefully

δ satisfies δ′ = δ² + χ — its slope is expressible using only δ itself. That is the entire trick. It means you can differentiate your guess as many times as you like and never produce a new kind of function. Everything stays a polynomial in δ, so "compare the coefficients" is a finite, well-defined thing to do. Choose a building block without that property and the method jams immediately.

Why the answer splits into two families

The little equation for δ has a constant χ in it, and its solution looks completely different depending on whether χ is negative or positive — cosh and sinh in one case, cos and sin in the other. So one calculation hands you two kinds of wave: a localised one and a repeating one. That is where your Uτ and Uυ come from.

Why N = 1 and not something else

The recipe could in principle be long — δ, δ², δ³ and so on. It has to stop somewhere, and where it stops is not a matter of taste. You count how fast each side of the equation grows: differentiating twice pushes the power up by two, while cubing pushes it up threefold. The only length at which those match is one term. So the recipe is short, and that is forced, not chosen.

The honest limitation: this finds solutions of the shape you assumed. It does not tell you they are the only ones, whether they are stable, or what happens for initial conditions of a different shape. What it does give — exact formulas, with no approximation anywhere — is rare enough to be worth having.

The method applies to any nonlinear PDE written as ℱ(U, Ux, Uy, Ut, Uxx, Uyy, Utt, …) = 0 with U = U(x, y, t), Eq. (5) in the report.

  1. Reduce the PDE to an ODE

    Assume the solution depends on the three variables only through one combination:

    U(x, y, t) = eiφ u(ξ) ξ = lx + my − ωt φ = −lx − my + ct
    (6)

    ξ is the travelling coordinate — the profile is constant along it. φ is the phase, carrying the oscillation. Note that they use the same l and m with opposite signs, and different speeds: ω for the envelope, c for the phase. That separation of group and phase velocity is standard for envelope equations, and it is what allows the imaginary part of the reduced equation to fix c independently.

    The result is an ODE in one variable, Eq. (7):

    𝒯(u, u′, u″, u‴, …) = 0
    (7)

  2. Expand the solution in an auxiliary function

    Assume u(ξ) is a finite Laurent-type series in a function δ(ξ):

    u(ξ) = A₀ + ΣNi=1 ( Ai δ−i(ξ) + Bi δi(ξ) )
    (8)

    The unified in "Unified Technique" is this: both positive and negative powers of δ appear. Methods that keep only positive powers (tanh-method, (G′/G)-expansion) are special cases with all Ai = 0. Including the negative powers is what lets one ansatz produce several known families at once.

    δ is not arbitrary — it satisfies a Riccati-type first-order ODE:

    δ′ = δ(ξ)² + χ
    (9)

    This is the engine of the method. Because δ′ is expressible in δ, every derivative of the series (8) is again a polynomial in δ. No new functions ever appear, however many times you differentiate — which is what makes coefficient matching possible at all.

    Eq. (9) is solved in closed form, and the sign of χ decides which family you get:

    χ < 0: δ(ξ) = [ √(χ(−(P²+S²))) − S√−χ · cosh(2√−χ (ξ+R)) ] / [ P + S·sinh(2√−χ (ξ+R)) ]
    (10)
    χ > 0: δ(ξ) = [ √(χ(S²−P²)) − S√χ · cos(2√χ (ξ+R)) ] / [ P + S·sin(2√χ (ξ+R)) ]
    (11)

    P, S and R are free constants of integration. R shifts the wave along ξ; P and S set its shape and, critically, whether the denominator can vanish.

  3. Fix N by homogeneous balancing

    N is not chosen by taste. Balance the highest-order derivative against the highest nonlinear term, counting powers of δ.

    In your equation the competition is u″ against u³. If u ~ δN, then since δ′ ~ δ² each differentiation raises the power by one: u′ ~ δN+1 and u″ ~ δN+2. Meanwhile u³ ~ δ3N. Setting them equal:

    N + 2 = 3N ⟹ N = 1

    That is a complete, self-contained derivation you can do on a whiteboard in fifteen seconds. Learn it — it is one of the most commonly asked questions on this family of methods, and being able to derive N rather than assert it is a visible difference in level.

  4. Substitute and match coefficients

    Put (8) and its derivatives, together with (9), into (7). Everything collapses to a single polynomial in δ(ξ). A polynomial vanishes identically only if every coefficient vanishes, so set the coefficient of each power δi to zero. The result is a system of nonlinear algebraic equations in Ai, Bi, ω and the equation's own parameters.

    The step converts a differential problem into an algebraic one. That is the trade the whole method exists to make.

  5. Solve the algebraic system

    Solve with a computer algebra system — your report used Maple. Substituting the resulting constants back into (8), with δ given by (10) or (11), produces the travelling-wave solutions.

    Expect to be asked about the CAS

    "Maple solved it" is a weak answer on its own. Be ready to say what the system looked like — five coupled polynomial equations in A₀, A₁, B₁ and ω — and how you handled the branches, since a nonlinear system has multiple roots and you reported one. See page 05, where the system is written out and the branch you took is identified.

Where this method sits

The Unified Technique belongs to a large family of ansatz-based exact-solution methods: the tanh and extended tanh methods, (G′/G)-expansion, the Kudryashov method, sine–cosine, the exp-function method, Jacobi elliptic function expansion. They share one architecture — assume a series in an auxiliary function with a known simple ODE, balance, match, solve.

StrengthLimitation
Exact closed-form solutions, with no discretisation error at all Only finds solutions of the assumed form; says nothing about the general solution
Fast, and mechanisable in a CAS No existence, uniqueness or stability information
Gives exact benchmarks against which a numerical scheme can be validated Cannot handle arbitrary initial or boundary conditions — the ansatz fixes them
Reveals parameter dependence analytically, not just sample by sample Nothing about what happens when the wave is perturbed, or how two of them interact

Say this if asked about limitations

"The method finds solutions of an assumed shape — it doesn't characterise the solution set, and it gives no stability information. What it does give is exact solutions, and those are genuinely useful as benchmarks: if you're validating a finite-difference or spectral solver for this equation, my Eq. (19) and (20) are exact initial data with a known exact answer at every later step, which is the cleanest possible convergence test."
