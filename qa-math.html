Q&A — general mathematics

Questions that are not about your report but sit one step around it. These separate candidates who know their own calculation from candidates who know the subject. Most panels spend more time here than on the thesis itself.

In plain words

These questions step one pace away from your thesis. The purpose is to find out whether you learned a subject or learned a calculation — because a research student will be handed problems that are not their thesis, and the panel needs to know you can move.

The four ideas everything here rests on

  • PDEs come in three flavours. Elliptic ones describe things that have settled down — the shape of a soap film, heat spread evenly over a plate. Parabolic ones describe smoothing and spreading, like heat flowing. Hyperbolic ones describe waves: things that travel, with a front and a finite speed. Yours is hyperbolic, and that is exactly why direction matters in it.
  • Dispersion is not diffusion. Both make a pulse spread out, and they are completely different. Diffusion damps it — the pulse fades away and the energy is gone. Dispersion just rearranges it — the pulse smears but keeps all its energy, because the components have merely drifted apart. The single letter i in the Schrödinger equation is what turns one into the other.
  • A soliton is a balance, not a shape. Spreading pulls one way, nonlinearity pulls the other, and when they cancel the pulse survives intact. The strict definition also demands that two of them can collide and come out unchanged — which is a much stronger claim, and one your project did not test.
  • Fractional derivatives are about memory. An ordinary derivative only looks at a point's immediate neighbourhood. A genuinely fractional one integrates over the entire history. That is why they turn up in materials that creep, and in diffusion that does not follow the usual rules.

The bridge they are really probing

If the group is computational, the questions will drift toward how you would compute this rather than solve it by hand. The honest and strong answer is that exact solutions like yours are what numerical methods get tested against. A solver is only trustworthy if you can point it at a problem whose answer is already known exactly and show that it agrees. Most equations have no such benchmark. Yours does — you produced it.

Where you have not used a method, say so in one sentence and then say what you have used. "I know the weak formulation but I've not implemented a finite-element solver" is a completely respectable answer. Bluffing is the only genuinely bad option, and it is always obvious.

PDE fundamentals

M01

Classify second-order PDEs. Where does your equation sit?

For Auxx + Buxy + Cuyy + … = 0 the discriminant B² − 4AC decides: negative is elliptic, zero parabolic, positive hyperbolic. My equation's spatial operator Uxx − Utt has A = 1, C = −1, B = 0, so the discriminant is +4 — hyperbolic.

The canonical examples, worth having ready:

  • Elliptic — Laplace, uxx + uyy = 0. Equilibrium, no time, boundary-value problems, infinitely smooth solutions.
  • Parabolic — heat, ut = uxx. Diffusive, smooths instantly, infinite propagation speed.
  • Hyperbolic — wave, utt = uxx. Finite propagation speed, characteristics, discontinuities persist.

One caveat worth adding: the full Schrödinger equation is first order in the evolution variable with an i in front, so it is dispersive rather than falling neatly into the three-way classification. The classification applies to the second-order spatial operator.

M02

What is dispersion, and how does it differ from diffusion?

Dispersion means different wavelengths travel at different speeds, so a packet spreads while its energy is conserved. Diffusion damps the amplitude and destroys energy. The i in the Schrödinger equation is exactly what turns one into the other.

Concretely: substitute u = ei(kx−ωt). For the heat equation ut = uxx you get ω = −ik², so the mode decays like e−k²t — diffusion, and high frequencies die fastest. For the free Schrödinger equation iut = −uxx you get ω = k², real, so the mode keeps its magnitude and only its phase rotates — dispersion.

The dispersion relation is the cleanest way to answer this, and being able to derive it in two lines is worth more than a verbal description.

M03

Derive the dispersion relation for the linear part of your equation.

Drop the cubic term, substitute U = ei(k₁x + k₂y − Ωt) into iUy + ½(Uxx − Utt) = 0 and read off the relation between k₂ and the other two.

Uy = ik₂U, Uxx = −k₁²U, Utt = −Ω²U. So i(ik₂) + ½(−k₁² + Ω²) = 0, giving k₂ = ½(Ω² − k₁²).

Note what that says: y is the evolution variable, and the "frequency" conjugate to it depends on the difference of the squares — a direct consequence of the hyperbolic sign. In the elliptic case it would be a sum, and the relation would be sign-definite.

M04

What is a well-posed problem?

Hadamard's three conditions: a solution exists, it is unique, and it depends continuously on the data.

The third is the one that bites in practice. If small changes in the initial data produce large changes in the solution, no numerical method can help you — the difficulty is in the problem, not the algorithm. The standard example is the backwards heat equation.

Relevant to your work: an ansatz-based method says nothing about well-posedness. You exhibit particular solutions; you do not show they are the only ones or that they are stable. Being able to say that unprompted is a mark of care.

Nonlinear waves

M05

What is a soliton, properly?

A localised travelling wave that keeps its shape, moves at constant speed, and survives collision with another one — emerging with its shape intact and only a phase shift.

The third condition is what distinguishes a soliton from a solitary wave, and it is tied to complete integrability — the existence of infinitely many conserved quantities and an inverse scattering transform. The classical integrable examples are KdV, the cubic NLS in 1+1 dimensions, and sine-Gordon.

History is worth a sentence: Russell observing a wave in the Union Canal in 1834, Korteweg and de Vries explaining it in 1895, and Zabusky and Kruskal coining "soliton" in 1965 after noticing the collision property numerically.

M06

Bright versus dark solitons?

A bright soliton is a localised peak on a zero background — sech-shaped, and it needs focusing nonlinearity. A dark soliton is a localised dip in a non-zero background — tanh-shaped, and it needs defocusing nonlinearity.

You should also know the third kind, because it comes up constantly in this literature: a singular soliton, where the denominator of the closed-form solution vanishes and the solution blows up.

Be careful not to assume your χ < 0 branch is one. It looks like it, but the apparent pole is removable and the solution is bounded on the real line — it is a kink, tending to ∓|B₁|√−χ at ξ → ±∞. See W07.

M07

Why does a soliton exist at all?

Exact balance between dispersion, which spreads a packet, and nonlinearity, which focuses it. Remove either and the balance fails.

In the NLS the intensity-dependent refractive index means an intense pulse creates its own guiding potential — self-phase modulation — and the resulting focusing exactly cancels the spreading from the second-derivative term. The balance is not approximate; it is why an exact solution exists.

M08

What is integrability, and is your equation integrable?

Integrability means the equation admits a Lax pair and an inverse scattering transform, with infinitely many conservation laws. I did not establish it for my equation, and I would not claim it.

The honest position: the 1+1-dimensional cubic NLS is integrable. Higher-dimensional and hyperbolic variants generally are not, and the fractional modification is a further complication. My method finds particular exact solutions, which is a much weaker statement than integrability — plenty of non-integrable equations have exact travelling-wave solutions.

Do not be tempted to claim integrability because you found exact solutions. They are not the same thing, and the difference is exactly what an examiner is testing.

Fractional calculus

M09

Name the main fractional derivatives and how they differ.

Riemann–Liouville and Caputo are genuinely non-local integral operators; conformable and beta are local operators built from a first derivative and a weight.

OperatorNon-local?Chain rule?Note
Riemann–LiouvilleYesNoDerivative of a constant is not zero
CaputoYesNoDerivative of a constant is zero; standard for initial-value problems
ConformableNoYesSimple, and criticised for that
Beta (Atangana)NoYesWhat I used; a weighted first derivative

The Caputo point about constants is the detail most often asked. Under Riemann–Liouville the fractional derivative of a constant is not zero, which makes physical initial conditions awkward; Caputo fixes that, which is why it dominates in applications.

M10

If you had to use Caputo instead, what would change?

The chain rule would be gone, so the travelling-wave reduction would fail and the whole method with it. I would have to solve numerically.

That is the real answer and it is a good one, because it identifies precisely what the method depends on. Concretely you would move to an L1 scheme or a Grünwald–Letnikov discretisation for the fractional operator, and immediately face the characteristic difficulty of non-local operators: every time step needs the entire history, so cost grows like O(N²) unless you use fast-convolution or sum-of-exponentials techniques.

Knowing that memory cost is the central practical issue in fractional numerics is the kind of detail that reads as genuine familiarity.

Bridging to numerics

M11

How would you solve your equation numerically?

Split-step Fourier: alternate between the linear part, which is diagonal in Fourier space, and the nonlinear part, which is diagonal in physical space and can be integrated exactly because |U| is constant over the nonlinear substep.

The structure: over a step h, advance the linear part by multiplying the Fourier coefficients by e−ik²h/2, then advance the nonlinear part in physical space by multiplying by ei|U|²h. Strang splitting — half a linear step, a full nonlinear step, half a linear step — makes it second order.

Why the nonlinear substep is exact: over that substep the equation is iUt = −|U|²U, and |U|² is conserved, so the solution is a pure phase rotation. That is the observation the method is built on.

Alternatives worth naming: Crank–Nicolson finite differences with a fixed-point iteration for the nonlinearity, or a relaxation scheme that conserves the discrete energy exactly.

M12

How would you check that a numerical solution is correct?

Three checks: convergence under refinement at the expected rate, conservation of quantities the equation conserves, and comparison against an exact solution — which is exactly what my project provides.

  • Convergence. Halve h, measure the error, confirm it falls by the expected factor. If you have no exact solution, compare against a much finer run.
  • Invariants. The NLS conserves mass ∫|U|² and an energy. Watching those drift is the cheapest possible diagnostic and it runs every step.
  • Exact benchmarks. Start from a known exact solution and measure the drift over time. My Eqs. (19) and (20) are precisely this.

The third point is your natural bridge from an analytical project into a computational group — say it in those terms.

M13

What is the CFL condition?

A necessary stability condition for explicit schemes on hyperbolic problems: the numerical domain of dependence must contain the physical one, which for the wave equation means cΔt/Δx ≤ 1.

The intuition is worth stating: if the physical wave travels further in one time step than the stencil can see, the scheme is computing without the information it needs, and it will blow up.

For dispersive problems like Schrödinger the analogous explicit restriction is harsher — Δt ~ Δx² — which is a large part of why implicit or spectral-splitting methods dominate there.

M14

Weak formulation and finite elements — what is the idea?

Multiply the equation by a test function, integrate over the domain, and move derivatives onto the test function by parts. This lowers the smoothness the solution needs and turns the PDE into a problem of finding a function in a finite-dimensional subspace.

The gain: a classical solution must be twice differentiable; a weak solution needs only one derivative in an L² sense, so H¹ is the natural space. Discretising means choosing a basis of piecewise polynomials on a mesh, which reduces everything to a linear system with a sparse matrix.

Be candid if you have not used FEM: "I know the formulation but I have not implemented a solver" is a perfectly respectable answer and far better than bluffing about a method you have not used. Then say what you have done.
