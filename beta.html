The beta fractional derivative

This is the part of your project most likely to draw a sceptical question, because the beta derivative is a local operator wearing the name "fractional". Know the criticism before it is put to you.

In plain words

Ordinary calculus only lets you differentiate a whole number of times. You can take one derivative, or two, or three. Fractional calculus asks a strange-sounding question: what would it mean to differentiate 0.8 times?

There are two reasons to care. First, some real systems genuinely sit between two integer-order behaviours — anomalous diffusion, viscoelastic materials, anything with memory. A first derivative is too little and a second is too much; the honest description is somewhere in between. Second, an adjustable order turns one model into a whole continuous family of models. Instead of a single equation you now have a dial, and you can turn it until the model matches your data.

Why "memory" keeps coming up

An ordinary derivative is local: to know the slope at a point, you only need to look right next to that point. A true fractional derivative is non-local: to get its value at a point you have to integrate over the whole history leading up to it. That is the mathematical shape of memory — the system's present behaviour depends on where it has been, not only on where it is now. Materials that creep, or particles that diffuse anomalously, behave like that.

Where the beta derivative fits — and the catch

The beta derivative is not one of the non-local ones. It is an ordinary first derivative multiplied by a factor that depends on where you are:

(beta derivative of f) = (x + 1/Γ(β))1−β × (ordinary derivative of f)

So it is local, and it does not really have memory. When β = 1 that factor becomes 1 and you get the plain derivative back, which is the sanity check you want. For other β it stretches the increment more the further out you are.

The reason to use it anyway is entirely practical, and it is worth stating plainly rather than dressing up: because it is local, the ordinary chain rule still works. The genuinely non-local operators — Caputo, Riemann–Liouville — do not have a simple chain rule, and without one, the substitution trick this whole project depends on is impossible. The beta derivative is the version of "fractional" that is weak enough to be solvable by hand.

That trade is the honest summary of this chapter: you buy exact closed-form solutions, and the price is that the operator does not model memory in the way the word "fractional" suggests. Knowing you paid that price — and being able to say what Caputo would have cost instead — is the difference between using a method and understanding it.

The definition

Due to Atangana et al., reference [7] in your report:

ᴬ₀𝔇βx f(x) = limh→0 [ f( x + g( x + 1/Γ(β) )1−β ) − f(x) ] / g ; 0 < β ≤ 1
(3)

Compare it with the ordinary derivative, which is the same limit with the increment being simply g. The beta derivative replaces that plain increment with one scaled by the point at which you are differentiating, through the factor (x + 1/Γ(β))1−β.

Two sanity checks worth having ready. At β = 1 the exponent 1 − β is zero, the factor becomes 1, and you recover the ordinary derivative exactly. As β decreases toward 0 the increment is stretched more and more strongly with x, so the operator weights distant behaviour differently — that is the "fractional" behaviour it is claiming.

The property that does all the work

Properties (iii) and (iv) are the interesting ones, and the reason is worth understanding rather than memorising: the Riemann–Liouville and Caputo fractional derivatives do not satisfy a simple product or chain rule. They are integral operators — genuinely non-local — and a product rule for them is an infinite series.

The beta derivative does satisfy them, because it is a local operator: a first derivative multiplied by a position-dependent factor. Concretely,

ᴬ₀𝔇βx f(x) = (x + 1/Γ(β))1−β · df/dx

And that identity is the entire reason the method works. It means a change of variable exists that converts the fractional PDE into an ordinary one — which is exactly what the transformation (12) does, with its (x + …)β/β terms. The fractional order does not survive into the ODE; it is absorbed into the definition of ξ.

This is the deep point of the whole project

The fractional derivative never has to be solved fractionally. It is removed by a substitution before any solving happens. If you can say that sentence and then show where it happens — the exponents on (x + 1/√β)β in Eq. (12) — you have demonstrated that you understand your own method rather than having followed a recipe.

Why use a fractional derivative at all

The motivations, in descending order of how well they hold up:

  1. Generalisation. β = 1 recovers the classical equation, so the fractional model contains the ordinary one as a special case and gives one extra parameter to fit against data.
  2. Memory and non-locality. The standard physical argument for fractional models: real media respond to their history, not only to the instantaneous state. Note that this argument is strongest for genuinely non-local operators.
  3. Tractability. Being honest — the beta derivative is chosen over Caputo or Riemann–Liouville largely because it admits a chain rule and therefore exact solutions. That is a legitimate reason, and stating it plainly is far better than pretending it was purely physical.

The honest critique — prepare for this

The question you may well get

"The beta derivative is just a first derivative multiplied by a function of x. It is local. Doesn't that mean your 'fractional' solutions are the ordinary solutions in a stretched coordinate?"

This is a real criticism in the literature, and it applies to the conformable derivative family generally. The honest answer is: substantially, yes — and knowing that is a strength, not a concession.

Say this

"That's a fair characterisation, and it's exactly why the method works — the beta derivative satisfies a chain rule, so the fractional PDE maps onto an ordinary one under the substitution in my Eq. (12). The solutions are the classical ones read in a deformed coordinate. What β buys you is a controlled deformation: in my Figure 9, increasing β narrows the wave and raises its frequency while the amplitude holds constant. Whether that deformation is the right physical model for memory in a fibre is a separate question, and I would not claim the beta derivative settles it — Caputo would be the operator to use if genuine non-locality mattered, at the cost of losing exact solvability."

The Γ(β) / √β inconsistency in your own report

Find this before an examiner does

Equation (3) defines the beta derivative using the factor (x + 1/Γ(β))1−β — with the gamma function, which is the standard definition. But the travelling-wave transformation in Equation (12) is written with (x + 1/√β)β — with a square root, not a gamma function. These do not agree.

Numerically at β = 0.8 they are not far apart — 1/Γ(0.8) ≈ 0.8935 against 1/√0.8 ≈ 1.1180 — so the figures would look qualitatively similar either way. But it is an inconsistency in the document, and the standard form in the literature is the gamma one.

If it is raised

"You're right, and I noticed it when I went back through the report. Equation (3) uses the standard Atangana definition with 1/Γ(β); the transformation in Eq. (12) is written with 1/√β. The gamma form is the correct one, and it is what I would use if I were rewriting this. At β = 0.8 the two constants differ by about 25%, which shifts the wave along ξ but does not change the structure of the solutions or any of the qualitative conclusions about θ and β. It is a transcription error, not a derivation error."

The Python lab on page 11 lets you set the convention with a flag and see the difference yourself, so this stops being an abstract worry.
