What the equation is, and why each term is there

You will be asked to explain your own governing equation term by term. This page is that explanation. It also settles the question that catches people out: what the word hyperbolic is doing in the title.

In plain words

Picture a short pulse of light travelling down a fibre-optic cable. Two things happen to it at once, and they pull in opposite directions.

It spreads out. A pulse is not one pure colour; it is a bundle of slightly different frequencies. In glass, different frequencies travel at slightly different speeds, so the bundle drifts apart as it goes. A sharp pulse becomes a smeared one. That is dispersion, and it is the enemy of sending information down a cable — smear it far enough and neighbouring pulses overlap and the bits are lost.

It squeezes itself back together. Glass is very slightly nonlinear: where the light is intense, the material's refractive index changes a little. So an intense pulse digs itself a shallow well and then sits in it. That is the Kerr effect, and it focuses the pulse.

A soliton is what happens when those two effects cancel exactly. The spreading and the squeezing balance, and the pulse travels a long way without changing shape at all. That is not a curiosity — it is the basis of soliton communication, where the pulse's own shape carries the bit and does not need to be regenerated every few kilometres.

What your equation is, then

It is the bookkeeping for that balance. Each term is one of the effects above: the second-derivative terms are the spreading, the |U|²U term is the squeezing, and the equation says they sum to zero — that is, they cancel.

And what "hyperbolic" adds

In the usual version of this equation the two spreading terms are added. In yours one is subtracted. That sounds like a footnote and is not. Adding them gives you the operator behind steady, settled problems — heat spread out over a plate, the shape of a soap film. Subtracting them gives you the operator behind waves: things that travel, with a direction and a speed and a wavefront.

The practical consequence for your project is that the direction the wave travels now matters. That is why an angle θ appears everywhere in your Chapter 4, and why "obliqueness" is a thing you could study at all. In the added-together version there would be no interesting angle to vary.

What "travelling wave" lets you do

Solving a PDE in three variables is hard. But if you assume the answer is a fixed shape sliding along at constant speed — the shape never changes, it just moves — then you can ride along with it. In that moving frame nothing changes at all, and three variables collapse into one. That single assumption is what makes the whole calculation possible.

The cost is worth being honest about: you are not finding all solutions of the equation, only the ones that happen to have that rigid sliding shape. Most solutions change form as they go, and this method cannot see them.

The equation

iUy + ½ (Uxx − Utt) + |U|²U = 0
(1)

U = U(x, y, t) is complex-valued. It is the slowly varying envelope of the optical field, not the field itself — the fast carrier oscillation has already been factored out, which is why the equation is first order in the evolution variable and why U is complex.

Term by term

TermNameWhat it does
iUy Evolution First order, with an i in front. The i is what makes the equation dispersive rather than diffusive: it rotates the phase instead of damping the amplitude. Note the evolution variable here is y, not t.
½Uxx Dispersion Spreads a pulse out. Different frequency components travel at different speeds, so a localised packet broadens.
−½Utt Dispersion, opposite sign The same second-derivative structure in t, but subtracted. This sign flip is the entire reason for the word "hyperbolic".
|U|²U Kerr nonlinearity Cubic, focusing. The medium's refractive index depends on intensity, so an intense pulse digs its own potential well and self-focuses.

The one-sentence physics

A soliton exists because the spreading from the dispersion terms and the focusing from the cubic term cancel exactly. Change either one and the balance breaks.

Why "hyperbolic" — the question people fumble

In the standard (2+1)-dimensional NLS the two second derivatives carry the same sign: Uxx + Uyy, an elliptic operator. Here they carry opposite signs: Uxx − Utt. That operator is the wave operator, and it is hyperbolic in the standard PDE classification. Hence "nonlinear hyperbolic Schrödinger equation".

The distinction is not cosmetic. It is also called the elliptic–hyperbolic or anomalous-dispersion distinction, and it changes the character of the solutions: the elliptic case is the focusing NLS with its familiar bright soliton, while the hyperbolic case supports X-wave and oblique structures — which is precisely why the oblique angle θ is a parameter worth varying in your Chapter 4.

Say this if asked "why hyperbolic?"

"Because the two second-order derivatives enter with opposite signs — Uxx minus Utt rather than plus. That makes the linear operator the wave operator, which is hyperbolic in the usual classification, rather than the Laplacian, which is elliptic. Physically it corresponds to anomalous dispersion in one of the two coordinates, and it is what makes oblique propagation an interesting parameter."

Solitons — what the word commits you to

A soliton, strictly, is a localised travelling wave that (i) keeps its shape, (ii) travels at constant speed, and (iii) survives collision with another one, emerging with only a phase shift. Condition (iii) is the strong one and is tied to complete integrability.

Be careful with the word

Your report says the Unified Technique is "an effective tool for deriving soliton wave solutions". What you actually derived are exact travelling-wave solutions of soliton type — you did not test collisions, and you did not establish integrability of this equation. If pressed, say "solitary wave" or "exact travelling-wave solution". That is the defensible claim, and volunteering the distinction reads as rigour rather than retreat.

Worth knowing about the two families you found:

  • χ < 0 → hyperbolic functions (cosh, sinh). On the real line this is a kink, not a peak: δ → ∓√−χ as ξ → ±∞, so |U| approaches the same plateau |B₁|√−χ on both sides with a localised feature in between. It is bounded — see the note below, which is a point worth being precise about.
  • χ > 0 → trigonometric functions (cos, sin). Periodic, not localised. Bounded wherever P + S·sin(·) stays away from zero, which for P > S it does. This is Figure 2.

The removable singularity — a detail worth owning

It looks as though Eq. (19) must blow up: its denominator P + S·sinh(a) vanishes when sinh(a) = −P/S, and with P = 3, S = 2 that happens at a real value of a. But the numerator vanishes at exactly the same point. It contains cosh(a), and it is zero when cosh(a) = √(P² + S²)/S. Those two conditions are compatible for every P and S, because

cosh²a − sinh²a = (P² + S²)/S² − P²/S² = 1

which is an identity, not a coincidence. So the singularity is removable: δ has a finite limit there, and on the real line |δ| ≤ √−χ, so |U| ≤ |B₁|√−χ.

That bound is not abstract — it is the plateau in your own figures. |B₁|√5 evaluates to 2.267, 1.948 and 1.500 at θ = 10°, 30° and 50°, and Figure 5 reads 2.28, 1.96 and 1.51 off the plot. At θ = 60° it gives 1.415 against Figure 10's ≈ 1.44.

Genuine poles do exist, but only at complex arguments — where sinh(a) = −P/S with Im(a) = π, at which the numerator is not zero. Those are reachable only where ξ itself is complex, that is where x < −1/√β and the transformation takes a fractional power of a negative number. The spikes in Figure 1 come from the branch cut of the transformation, not from the solution being singular. See page 09.

The travelling-wave idea

The whole method rests on one assumption: that a solution exists which depends on x, y and t only through a single combination ξ = lx + my − ωt, multiplied by a phase.

U(x, y, t) = eiφ u(ξ)
(2)

Geometrically: the solution is a fixed profile u sliding rigidly through space. In the frame moving with the wave nothing changes. That is what reduces three independent variables to one and turns the PDE into an ODE — the single most important step in the report, and the first thing to be able to justify.

The cost is equally important to state: this is an ansatz, not a derivation. You are not finding all solutions of the PDE. You are finding the ones of this particular shape. Solutions that change form as they propagate — which is most of them — are outside what the method can see.

The physical reading

The report motivates the equation as a model for "how the optics signal spreads in single-mode fibre optics". Concretely: an optical pulse travelling down a fibre broadens because of dispersion and self-focuses because of the Kerr effect. When those balance, the pulse propagates undistorted — the physical basis of soliton communication, where the pulse shape itself carries the bit and does not need periodic reshaping.

The parameters you vary have readings too. θ, through l = cos θ and m = sin θ, is the direction the wave travels relative to the axes — the obliqueness. β is the fractional order, which the report interprets as memory or non-local response in the medium. And χ selects which family of auxiliary solutions you land in, and so whether the result is localised or periodic.
