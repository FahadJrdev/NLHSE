Hard questions & honest gaps

Everything on this page is a real weakness in the report. None of it is fatal. All of it is survivable if you get there first — and one item, the ω error, converts from a liability into the strongest thing you can say, because you found it and fixed it yourself.

The principle

An examiner who finds a flaw you did not know about learns that you do not know your own work. An examiner who finds a flaw you name first, diagnose exactly, and have already tested learns the opposite. The flaws are fixed; only the order of discovery is still under your control.

In plain words

Every piece of work has weak points. Yours has four or five, and none of them is serious. What decides how much they cost you is not the flaws themselves — those are already fixed in the document, unchangeable — but who finds them first.

If an examiner finds a problem you did not know about, what they learn is that you do not know your own work. If you name it yourself, explain exactly what went wrong, and show you have already tested the fix, what they learn is the opposite: that you check things, that your claims can be trusted, and that you are the sort of person who finds errors rather than the sort who ships them. Same flaw, opposite conclusion.

The big one, in plain terms

One of the constants printed in your report is wrong. Not the mathematics — the printing. A χ that should have cancelled didn't, so the published ω is too big by a factor of √χ.

Here is why that is a good story rather than a bad one. The other constant, B₁, is correct — and B₁ and ω have to satisfy a simple relationship that comes straight out of your own equations. Testing that relationship is how the error shows up, and it takes about a minute. The reason nobody caught it in 2025 is that the report never substitutes the finished answer back into the original equation. That check is the most basic quality control there is, and its absence is the real lesson — not the typo.

So the sentence you want is roughly: "There's a typo in my printed ω. I found it by doing the back-substitution the report doesn't contain. B₁ is right, the correction is one factor of root chi, and with it the solution satisfies the equation to numerical round-off — I can show you." That is a candidate who improved their own work after submitting it.

The other four, briefly

  • Two symbols disagree between one equation and another — a slip, no consequence.
  • A phase factor gets dropped between the setup and the final answer — harmless for the plots, but incomplete.
  • The figures don't record what value of y was used, so nobody can reproduce them exactly. That is a genuine reproducibility gap.
  • The spikes in Figure 1 are not what they look like. Explained in W07 — worth reading, because the obvious answer is wrong.

The last category on this page is different: not errors, but questions about fit — why an analytical mathematician wants a numerical position, and why someone who has been writing software for six years is coming back to research. Those need answers too, and they need to be true ones.

Errors in the report

W01

Your printed ω does not satisfy your own algebraic system. Explain.

Correct — it is a typesetting error. One χ too many survived inside the radical. B₁ is right; ω as printed is too large by a factor of √χ, and with the corrected value the solution satisfies the equation to numerical round-off.

The diagnosis, precisely. With A₀ = A₁ = 0 the trial solution is u = B₁δ, and since δ′ = δ² + χ we get u″ = 2B₁δ³ + 2B₁χδ. Substituting into the real part (14) and collecting powers of δ gives exactly two conditions:

δ³ : B₁(l² − ω²) + B₁³ = 0 ⟹ B₁² = ω² − l² δ¹ : χ(l² − ω²) + ζ = 0 ⟹ ζ = χ(ω² − l²)

Those are equations 1 and 3 of the report's own system (18), so the system is right. Testing the printed constants against the first one at θ = 60°, χ = −5: ω² − l² = 0.5022 but B₁² = −0.4004. They do not match.

The fix. The report prints ω = ½√(χ(2χl² − l² + 2m + √Δ)/χ), in which the χ cancels. The correct expression has no leading χ:

ω = ½ √( (2χl² − l² + 2m + √Δ) / χ )

With that, ω² − l² = B₁² exactly, for both χ = −5 and χ = +5, and the residual of Eq. (14) drops from order one to about 10−6 — which is just the truncation error of the finite difference used to test it.

Be precise about what this does and does not establish

It establishes that the printed ω is inconsistent with the report's own system, and that a specific one-character correction removes the inconsistency. It does not establish which value the original Maple worksheet used, because the figures depend on ω, on y and on the Γ(β)/√β choice together, and the report fixes none of the three. Say the first part with confidence and the second part as the open question it is.

Say this

"There's a typo in the printed expression for ω — one χ too many survived inside the radical. I found it when I went back and substituted the solution into the equation, which the report itself never does. B₁ is correct as printed; the correct ω is one over root chi times what's written. With that correction the solution satisfies the reduced ODE to numerical round-off. B₁ is independently confirmed by the figures — the plateau of the χ < 0 branch is |B₁|√−χ, and that comes out at 2.267, 1.948 and 1.500 for the three angles in Figure 5, which reads 2.28, 1.96 and 1.51. So it's a transcription error in one line rather than a mistake in the algebra."

You can demonstrate this live

Cell 2 of the Python lab prints the residual under both values. It takes about ten seconds to run. If the conversation is technical and going well, offering to show it is a strong move.

W02

Equation (3) uses 1/Γ(β) but Equation (12) uses 1/√β. Which is right?

Γ(β) is right. The √β in the transformation is a transcription slip, and the cancellation that makes the whole method work only happens with the gamma form.

The mechanism: the beta derivative is (x + 1/Γ(β))1−β d/dx. Applying it to (1/β)(x + 1/Γ(β))β multiplies (x + …)1−β by (x + …)β−1, which is exactly 1. That is why ᴬ₀𝔇βξ = l and why β vanishes from the reduced ODE. With 1/√β inside instead, the exponents no longer cancel cleanly.

The practical impact is small: at β = 0.8, 1/Γ(0.8) ≈ 0.8935 against 1/√0.8 ≈ 1.1180. The wave shifts along ξ; nothing structural changes, and no conclusion about θ or β is affected.

W04

Where did the eiφ go? Eq. (2) has it, Eqs. (19) and (20) do not.

It was dropped in the final write-up. For plots of |U| it is harmless when φ is real, which is why the figures are still reproducible from the printed formulas — but the complete solution should carry it.

The full solution is U = eiφB₁δ(ξ) with φ given in (12). Since |eiφ| = 1 for real φ, every figure in Chapter 4 is unaffected. Only the phase structure is lost, and with it any discussion of the phase velocity c — which is presumably why c never reappears after Eq. (16).

The report does not record it. Every figure is drawn over x and t, so y is held at some fixed value that should have been stated in the captions.

Say it plainly; there is no good way to bluff a number you do not have. W07

Your Figure 1 shows spikes reaching the top of the axis. Is that solution bounded?

On the real line the solution is bounded — the apparent singularity is removable. The spikes come from the region where the transformation makes ξ complex, which is a property of the beta substitution rather than of the solution.

Get this right; the obvious answer is wrong. It looks as though Eq. (19) must blow up where its denominator P + S·sinh(a) = 0, i.e. sinh(a) = −P/S. But the numerator contains cosh(a) and vanishes when cosh(a) = √(P²+S²)/S — and those two conditions always coincide, because cosh²a − sinh²a = 1 gives (P²+S²)/S² − P²/S² = 1 identically. So the limit is finite. On the real line |δ| ≤ √−χ and therefore |U| ≤ |B₁|√−χ.

That bound is checkable against your own figures: |B₁|√5 is 2.267, 1.948 and 1.500 at θ = 10°, 30° and 50°, and Figure 5 reads 2.28, 1.96 and 1.51. The plateau in the figures is the bound.

So where do the spikes come from? There are genuine poles, but only at complex arguments — sinh(a) = −P/S also has solutions with Im(a) = π, and there the numerator is not zero. Those are reachable only where ξ itself is complex, that is where x < −1/√β ≈ −1.12 and (x + 1/√β)β is a fractional power of a negative number. Every figure showing spikes plots x well below that threshold.

Say this

"The solution is bounded wherever ξ is real — the singularity you'd expect from the denominator is removable, because the numerator vanishes at the same point; that follows from cosh² − sinh² = 1. The bound is |B₁|√−χ, and that's exactly the plateau in Figures 5 and 10. The spikes appear only for x below −1/√β, where the beta transformation takes a fractional power of a negative number and ξ goes complex. So they're an artefact of the transformation's branch cut rather than a feature of the wave — which I'd argue is an issue with how far the plotting range was extended."

That last observation is a genuine criticism of the report that you are making of your own work, which is the strongest position to be in.

W08

Are these solutions stable?

I did not investigate stability, and the method gives no information about it.

Then show you know what the investigation would be: linearise about the solution, substitute U + εv, keep terms of first order in ε, and look for eigenvalues of the resulting linear operator with positive real part. Numerically you would evolve the exact solution with a small perturbation added and watch whether it grows.

You can also make a physical prediction: a singular solution is not a candidate for a stable physical state, so the χ < 0 branch would be the one to expect trouble from.

W09

Isn't this just applying a known method to another equation?

Substantially yes, and that is what an M.S. project is for. The method is cited; the application, the algebra and the parameter analysis are mine.

Do not oversell. Say what is genuinely there: a specific equation, carried through with a specific fractional operator, with a parameter study that produces a clear and checkable claim — β rescales without touching amplitude, and it cannot do otherwise because β never enters B₁.

If they press on novelty, the honest framing is that the contribution is incremental and the value is in the training: I can take a nonlinear PDE, reduce it, solve it, and now also verify it numerically.

Positioning

W10

This is analytical work. We are a numerical group. Why should we take you?

Name the gap in one sentence, then show the bridge is already half built.

Say this

"You're right that my project was analytical — I derived exact solutions, I didn't discretise anything. Three things transfer. First, the PDE background is the same: classification, dispersion relations, what the nonlinearity does. Second, exact solutions are what you validate numerical schemes against, and mine are usable that way. Third, I've since implemented the solutions in Python, reproduced my own figures, and in doing it found and corrected an error in the published constants — so I've already done the numerical verification the report was missing. What I haven't done is write a solver for this equation, and that's what I'd want to start with."

The last sentence matters. Naming what you have not done, precisely, makes everything before it credible.

W11

Your degree is in mathematics but you have been working as a software developer. Why the switch back?

Answer it as a combination rather than a reversal — the programming is an asset in computational science, not a detour away from it.

The credible version: six years of professional software engineering means you write code that other people can run, you use version control properly, you test, and you can handle data and performance work. Those are exactly the skills most maths graduates entering a computational group lack, and supervisors know it.

Be straightforward about motivation and do not invent a grand narrative. A plain, true reason delivered directly is more convincing than a rehearsed story.

W12

A question you genuinely cannot answer.

"I don't know" — then say what you would do to find out, or what you do know that is adjacent.

Panels are not testing whether you know everything; they are testing what you do at the edge of your knowledge, because that is where a research student lives. A confident wrong answer is much worse than an honest gap, and it is usually obvious.

The shape of a good non-answer

"I haven't worked with that. What I do know is [adjacent thing], and I'd expect [reasoned guess] — but I'd want to check that rather than assert it."
