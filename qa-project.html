Opening questions

P01

Tell me about your master's project.

I derived exact travelling-wave solutions of the (2+1)-dimensional nonlinear hyperbolic Schrödinger equation under a beta fractional derivative, using the Unified Technique, and studied how obliqueness and the fractional order reshape them.

Then stop and let them steer. If they want more: the equation models optical signal propagation in single-mode fibre. The method turns the PDE into an ODE through a travelling-wave ansatz, expands the solution as a short series in an auxiliary function satisfying δ′ = δ² + χ, and matches coefficients to get an algebraic system. Two closed-form families come out — hyperbolic for χ < 0 and trigonometric for χ > 0.

Do not recite the whole report unprompted. Two sentences, then a pause, is a stronger opening than ninety seconds of monologue.

P02

Why is it called hyperbolic?

Because the two second derivatives enter with opposite signs — Uxx − Utt — which makes the linear operator the wave operator, hyperbolic in the standard classification, rather than the Laplacian, which is elliptic.

In the ordinary (2+1)-dimensional NLS you would have Uxx + Uyy. The sign flip is not cosmetic: it corresponds to anomalous dispersion in one coordinate, and it is what makes oblique propagation a meaningful parameter to study — which is why θ appears throughout my Chapter 4.

P03

Walk me through the derivation.

Transformation, split, balance, match, solve — five moves.

  1. Assume U = eiφu(ξ) with ξ a beta-deformed combination of x, y and t. This reduces the PDE to a single ODE.
  2. u is real, so the real and imaginary parts of the ODE must vanish separately. The imaginary part is linear and gives the speed immediately: c = (l² − m)/ω.
  3. Balance u″ against u³: since δ′ = δ², each derivative raises the power by one, so N + 2 = 3N and N = 1.
  4. Substitute u = A₀ + A₁/δ + B₁δ and match powers of δ. A₀ = 0 falls straight out of the 3A₀B₁² = 0 equation.
  5. Solve the remaining system for ω and B₁, and substitute the two branches of δ — cosh/sinh for χ < 0, cos/sin for χ > 0.

Ask for a whiteboard. This answer is much stronger written than spoken.

The method

P04

Why N = 1? Where does that come from?

Homogeneous balancing: if u ~ δN then u″ ~ δN+2 because δ′ = δ² + χ raises the power by one per derivative, while u³ ~ δ3N. Setting N + 2 = 3N gives N = 1.

The important part is why differentiating raises the power. Because δ′ is itself quadratic in δ, differentiating δN gives NδN−1δ′ = NδN−1(δ² + χ), whose leading term is δN+1. Do it twice and you are at δN+2.

Derive it on the board rather than asserting it. It takes fifteen seconds and it is visibly different from having memorised a rule.

P05

What makes the Unified Technique "unified"?

The ansatz carries both positive and negative powers of the auxiliary function, so one expansion reproduces several established methods as special cases.

Keep only positive powers and you have essentially the tanh or (G′/G) method. Including Aiδ−i alongside Biδi lets a single calculation generate solutions those methods would need separate runs to find.

Be honest about how it turned out here: A₁ came out zero, so for this equation the negative-power half contributed nothing and the result sits in the positive-power branch. The general ansatz was used; the equation collapsed it. Saying that is better than letting them find it.

P06

What is δ, and why that particular ODE?

δ is an auxiliary function satisfying δ′ = δ² + χ — a Riccati equation — chosen because it is solvable in closed form and because it keeps every derivative of the ansatz polynomial in δ.

That closure property is the whole point. Differentiate the series any number of times and no new functions appear; everything stays a polynomial in δ. That is what makes "collect coefficients and set them to zero" a finite, well-defined operation.

The sign of χ picks the branch of the Riccati solution: negative gives hyperbolic functions, positive gives trigonometric ones. That single sign is what produces two qualitatively different solution families from one calculation.

P07

You used Maple. What did it actually do?

It solved the nonlinear algebraic system that comes out of coefficient matching, and it produced the figures. The setting up of the system and the choice of which root to keep were mine.

Be able to describe the system: with A₀ = A₁ = 0 it reduces to two conditions — B₁(l² − ω²) + B₁³ = 0, which gives B₁² = ω² − l², and χ(l² − ω²) + ζ = 0. Those two in the unknowns ω and B₁, with ζ depending on ω, give a quadratic in ω² and hence multiple roots.

"Maple solved it" alone is a weak answer. "Maple solved a system I can write down, and here are the two conditions" is a strong one.

The fractional derivative

P08

Why a fractional derivative? What does β buy you?

It generalises the model — β = 1 recovers the classical equation — and gives one extra parameter that deforms the wave's spatial scale without touching its amplitude.

The physical motivation usually given is memory or non-local response in the medium. The mathematical motivation, which I would state plainly, is that the beta derivative admits a chain rule, and that is what makes exact solutions reachable at all.

What β actually does is visible in my Figure 9: three values of β give curves with identical peak height and different spacing. That is not a coincidence — β appears only inside ξ, never in B₁, so it is mathematically incapable of changing the amplitude.

P09

The beta derivative is local. Isn't that a contradiction in terms for a "fractional" derivative?

It is a fair criticism, and the honest answer is that the beta derivative is a first derivative multiplied by a position-dependent factor — so my solutions are the classical ones read in a deformed coordinate.

Concretely, ᴬ₀𝔇βxf = (x + 1/Γ(β))1−β f′(x). That locality is exactly why the chain rule survives, and why my transformation (12) can absorb the fractional order entirely — β does not appear anywhere in the reduced ODE (13).

If genuine non-locality mattered — a real memory kernel — the operator to use would be Caputo, and exact solvability would be lost; you would be solving numerically. That is a legitimate trade, and I would not claim the beta derivative settles the physics.

Do not get defensive here. Conceding a good criticism accurately is what separates a candidate who understands the work from one who performed it.

P10

Why does β disappear from your ODE?

Because the transformation is built precisely to cancel it: applying the beta derivative to (1/β)(x + 1/Γ(β))β multiplies (x + …)1−β by (x + …)β−1, which is 1.

So ᴬ₀𝔇βxξ = l exactly as ∂ξ/∂x = l would be classically. The fractional order is not solved for; it is absorbed into the definition of the travelling coordinate before any solving happens.

This is the single best thing you can say about the structure of your own derivation.

The results

P11

What did you actually find? Give me the result, not the method.

Two exact solution families, and a clear parameter picture: obliqueness trades amplitude for width, while the fractional order rescales the wave horizontally and leaves the amplitude untouched.

Quantitatively, from Figure 6: peak |U| falls from about 1.85 at θ = 10° to 1.17 at 20° and 0.65 at 30°. From Figure 9: three values of β give identical peak heights with different spacing.

Numbers beat adjectives. "The amplitude decreases with obliqueness" is a weaker answer than "it falls from 1.85 to 0.65 between ten and thirty degrees."

P12

Are these solitons?

They are exact travelling-wave solutions of soliton type. I would not claim soliton in the strict sense, because I did not test collision behaviour or establish integrability.

The strict definition requires that two of them collide and emerge unchanged apart from a phase shift. My work does not test that. "Solitary wave" or "exact travelling-wave solution" is the defensible term.

There is a sharper point available. The χ < 0 family is a kink on the real line, not a peak: δ → ∓√−χ as ξ → ±∞, so |U| tends to the same plateau |B₁|√−χ on both sides. It is bounded — the apparent pole in its denominator is removable, because the numerator vanishes at the same point by the identity cosh² − sinh² = 1. The spikes in my Figure 1 appear only where ξ turns complex, below x = −1/√β. The χ > 0 family is bounded and periodic.

Volunteering this is a strong move. It is precise, it is checkable, and it shows you read your own figures rather than describing them. See W07 for the full version.

P13

Did you verify that your solutions satisfy the original equation?

Not in the report, and that is a gap. I have since done it: substituting back gives a residual at the level of numerical round-off, and the check also turned up a typesetting error in one of my printed constants.

The honest sequence: the report contains no back-substitution check and no residual plot. Going back to it, u = B₁δ satisfies the reduced real ODE to about 10−6 — pure finite-difference error — once ω is corrected. As printed, ω is too large by a factor of √χ, and the residual is order one.

See page 09. This is the most valuable single thing in your preparation: an error you found yourself, diagnosed exactly, and can demonstrate live in the Python lab.

P14

Why do the square roots produce imaginary numbers?

Because at these parameter values the radicands genuinely are negative — B₁ ≈ 0.633i at θ = 60°, and √(−5P² + 5S²) = 5i at P = 3, S = 2. U is complex-valued anyway, so this is consistent; the figures plot |U|.

It is worth being explicit that this is expected rather than a bug: the governing equation is a Schrödinger equation and U is a complex envelope from the outset. What you should be able to say is that the modulus is what is physically meaningful — the intensity — and that is what every figure shows.

The practical consequence, if you are asked how you would compute with these: you must work in complex arithmetic throughout. In NumPy that means np.emath.sqrt rather than np.sqrt, which would return nan.

P15

What do P, S and R mean physically?

They are constants of integration from the Riccati equation, not physical parameters. R is a pure translation along ξ; P and S set the shape.

The interesting one is the P–S relationship, and it behaves differently on the two branches. For the χ > 0 branch the denominator is P + S·sin(·), whose minimum on the real line is P − S; with P = 3 and S = 2 that is 1, so it never vanishes. Make S larger than P and that family develops genuine real singularities.

The χ < 0 branch is different: there the zero of the denominator is always cancelled by a zero of the numerator, for every P and S, so it is bounded on the real line whatever you choose.

I held them fixed at 3, 2 and 3 throughout, which in hindsight is the most interesting experiment the report did not run.

Context and judgement

P16

What is this useful for?

Two things: the physical model is optical pulse propagation in fibre, and the exact solutions are benchmarks for validating numerical solvers.

The physics: dispersion spreads a pulse, the Kerr nonlinearity focuses it, and when they balance the pulse propagates without distortion. That is the basis of soliton communication.

The second use is the one to lead with in a computational group. An exact solution is exact initial data with a known exact answer at every later time — the cleanest possible convergence test for a finite-difference or spectral scheme. Most PDEs have no such benchmark.

P17

What was the hardest part?

Keeping the algebra straight through the substitution — the expansion of u″ with δ′ = δ² + χ produces a lot of terms, and a single sign error propagates silently to the end.

A more interesting version of the same answer: the hardest part in hindsight was that I had no independent check. The algebra was verified by being careful rather than by being tested. That is exactly the gap I closed afterwards by substituting the solutions back numerically — and it is how I found the ω error.

Answering with a real difficulty and what you did about it is far stronger than "time management".

P18

What would you do differently?

Four things: verify by back-substitution, vary P and S, record the value of y used in the plots, and add a numerical solution of the PDE to compare against.

  • Verification. A residual plot belongs in the report. Its absence is what let the ω typo through.
  • P and S. Fixed at 3 and 2 in all eleven figures, yet they decide whether the solution is bounded. Varying them is the obvious missing experiment.
  • The value of y. Every figure is drawn over x and t, so y is fixed at some value the report never states.
  • A numerical comparison. Solve the PDE with a split-step Fourier scheme from my exact solution as initial data, and measure the drift. My own conclusion names this as the next step.
